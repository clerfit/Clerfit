Scientific References

Primary studies, public-health guidance and recognised technical standards.

How to use this reference library

These sources inform selected equations, measurement methods and educational context used across ClerFit. A citation is not proof that every calculator implementation has been independently validated, and it does not mean the cited organisation endorses ClerFit. Check the individual calculator page for its exact equation, input requirements and limitations.

Where a method is descriptive or site-defined and has no accepted clinical standard, it should be presented as arithmetic or a measurement comparison—not as a diagnosis, universal health category or professional assessment.

Body measurements and health screening

  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) — Adult BMI Categories. Adult BMI categories and BMI calculation method. BMI is a screening measure and does not directly measure body fat or distinguish muscle from fat.
  • CDC — Child and Teen BMI. Age- and sex-specific BMI-for-age guidance. Adult BMI categories are not interchangeable with paediatric percentiles.
  • U.S. Department of Defense circumference-based body-fat equations. A National Academies review describes the equations used in the cited Navy and Marine Corps framework: Assessing Fitness for Military Enlistment. Worked examples also appear in the official U.S. Army body-fat calculation guidance. Service-specific procedures and standards can change; ClerFit's result is an educational estimate, not an official military assessment.
  • Woolcott & Bergman (2018). “Relative fat mass (RFM) as a new estimator of whole-body fat percentage.” Scientific Reports. Read the study.
  • Krakauer & Krakauer (2012). “A New Body Shape Index Predicts Mortality Hazard Independently of Body Mass Index.” PLOS ONE. Read the paper.
  • NICE guideline NG246. Identifying and assessing overweight, obesity and central adiposity, including adult waist-to-height interpretation and applicability.
  • Heath & Carter (1967). “A modified somatotype method.” American Journal of Physical Anthropology. Publication DOI. Somatotype is a specific anthropometric method, not a universal health or performance rating.
  • Jackson & Pollock (1978); Jackson, Pollock & Ward (1980). Adult skinfold equations for predicting body density. Men’s equation and women’s equation. These are population-derived estimates and require the original measurement protocol.
  • Durnin & Womersley (1974). Body fat assessed from body density and estimated from skinfold thickness. British Journal of Nutrition article. The implemented coefficients cover the reported adult age groups.
  • Bergman et al. (2011); subsequent independent evaluation (2012). The original Body Adiposity Index paper and an independent evaluation for cardiovascular-risk prediction. BAI remains an estimate with population and sex-related limitations.

Energy expenditure and exercise

  • Mifflin et al. (1990). “A new predictive equation for resting energy expenditure in healthy individuals.” American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. PubMed record. This equation estimates resting energy expenditure; it is not a direct metabolic measurement.
  • Mosteller (1987). “Simplified calculation of body-surface area.” New England Journal of Medicine. PubMed record.
  • FAO / WHO / UNU. Human Energy Requirements. Physical activity level definitions and adult energy requirements.
  • Tanaka, Monahan & Seals (2001). “Age-predicted maximal heart rate revisited.” Journal of the American College of Cardiology. PubMed record. Age-based maximum-heart-rate equations have individual error.
  • 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities. Published methods paper and Compendium resource. MET values depend on activity definitions and are not direct measurements of an individual's energy expenditure.
  • Cooper 12-minute run field-test literature. Example validation literature. A field-test VO₂ estimate is not equivalent to laboratory gas analysis.
  • Strength prediction research. Published comparisons of repetition-based one-repetition-maximum equations include comparative equation research and later validation literature. Predicted 1RM depends on exercise, technique and population.

Nutrition and dietary reference values

  • National Academies (2005). Dietary Reference Intakes for Energy, Carbohydrate, Fiber, Fat, Fatty Acids, Cholesterol, Protein, and Amino Acids. Publication page.
  • National Academies (2019). Dietary Reference Intakes for Sodium and Potassium. Summary and Potassium chapter.
  • National Institutes of Health, Office of Dietary Supplements. Dietary supplement fact sheets provide evidence summaries and reference information for individual nutrients.
  • Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics. Evidence-based position paper on dietary fiber, indexed at PubMed.

Public health, sleep and environmental guidance

  • World Health Organization (WHO). Physical activity fact sheet and Ultraviolet (UV) Index guidance.
  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Sleep and health guidance, summarising age-based recommendations.
  • U.S. National Weather Service. Heat Index and Wind Chill FAQ. These indices do not guarantee a safe exposure duration.
  • National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). Guide to the SI, Appendix B and unit conversion guidance.

Custom and descriptive calculations

Some calculators use direct arithmetic, geometric approximations or site-defined comparisons for which no single accepted clinical reference range exists. Examples include certain facial ratios, posture comparisons, fatigue indicators, session-density measures and personal load ratios. These tools should disclose their equations and avoid implying a validated diagnosis, universal norm or attractiveness standard.

For route-specific methods and limitations, read the explanation displayed on the individual calculator page.
